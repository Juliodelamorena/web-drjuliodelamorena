Exercise plan · Hip

Exercises for greater trochanteric pain (gluteal tendinopathy)

A guide only. It does not replace a medical assessment or your physiotherapist's judgement. If the pain clearly increases, stop and seek advice.
Why this plan

The gluteus medius and gluteus minimus are the muscles that hold up the pelvis every time you stand on one foot: when walking, climbing a step or getting up from a chair. Their tendons end at the bone you can feel on the side of the hip, the greater trochanter. When those tendons suffer, the pain appears right there, on the outer side, and gets worse with long periods sitting, getting up, walking uphill or lying on that side. It is what is often called trochanteritis or trochanteric bursitis.

These tendons tolerate load well but compression very badly. Every time the leg crosses inwards — crossing your legs, sleeping on your side with the top knee dropped, standing with your weight hanging on one hip — the tendon is squashed against the bone. That repeated compression, more than effort, is what keeps it irritated.

This gives the rule that governs the whole plan: load yes, compression no. Rest does not work, because a weak gluteus lets the pelvis drop with every step and the tendon is compressed again. The plan adds lower-back control and hip mobility: a stiff hip forces the spine to move in its place.

The golden rule

Load is adjusted according to the pain the following day, not the same day. If the next morning the hip hurts more than before the session, you overdid it: rest that day and do half at the next session. If you are the same or better, carry on and increase. Discomfort of up to 3 out of 10 during exercise is acceptable if it goes away when you finish.

Before you start
  • Always warm up, even for 5 minutes. Exercise bike at low resistance or brisk walking. A cold tendon tolerates much less load. Saddle high: a low one closes the hip and compresses the outer side
  • Neutral lower back. Neither arched nor sunken in any exercise. If your back arches to complete a movement, reduce the range
  • Consistency before intensity. Five short sessions a week achieve much more than two long ones
  • Order matters. First mobility and lower-back control, then glutes and finally the standing exercises. When you start a new phase, the isometrics from the first become your warm-up

Phase 1 · Activation without compression

Weeks 1 and 2 · Every day · 15 minutes

The aim of this phase is to settle the tendon and regain control. The glute work is isometric: you push without moving the joint. A sustained contraction has an immediate pain-relieving effect on the tendon and prepares it for load. Moderate intensity: you should be able to hold it without shaking.

  1. Lying on your back, knees bent. As you breathe out, gently draw your navel towards your spine without moving the pelvis or squeezing the glutes. Keep the activation while breathing normally. It is the basis of all the lower-back work.

    10 × 10 s · every day

  2. Lying on your back, knees bent and feet flat hip-width apart, with an elastic band around the thighs just above the knees. Push the knees outwards against the band without actually moving them, and hold. Pelvis still, back neutral.

    5 × 30-45 s (1 min rest) · every day

  3. Same position, with the band on. Activate the transversus, push the knees against the band and lift the pelvis until knees, hips and shoulders are in line. Hold for 3 seconds at the top and lower slowly. Do not arch your back as you rise: the height is set by the pelvis, not the lower back.

    3 × 10 · 3 s pause at the top · every day

  4. Lying on your back, bring both knees towards your chest with your hands, without pulling hard. Hold while breathing. It opens up the lower back and offloads it.

    3 × 20-30 s · every day

  5. Lying on your back, knees bent and together. Let them drop gently to one side and then the other, without your shoulders leaving the floor. Short range at first: if you feel pressure on the side of the hip, reduce it.

    2 × 10 each side · every day

  6. On all fours, hands under shoulders and knees under hips. Round your back towards the ceiling and then bring it back to neutral. Do not let the lower back sag at the end: forced spinal extension adds nothing here.

    2 × 10 · every day

Phase 2 · Progressive load and mobility

Weeks 3 to 6 · 4 days a week · 20 minutes

This is where the work that changes the tendon begins. You move from sustained contraction to loaded movement, always slow and controlled: 2 seconds up, 1 pause, 3 down. The Phase 1 isometrics stay as your warm-up. Criterion to start: two weeks completing Phase 1 without worsening the next day.

  1. Lying on your side on the hip that hurts less, with a pillow under the waist and another between the knees. Bottom leg bent. Lift the straight top leg, with the foot slightly back and the toes pointing forwards, only to horizontal and a little higher. Lower to the pillow, never below it: the leg must not cross the body's midline.

    3 × 10-12 · tempo 2-1-3 · 4 days/week

  2. Back against the wall, feet hip-width apart and a step forward. Slide down to a comfortable knee angle, no more than 60°, and come back up. The knees follow the direction of the feet without caving in.

    3 × 10 · 4 days/week

  3. Standing facing a support, knees slightly bent. Push the hips back, tilting the trunk with a straight back, as if closing a door with your bottom, and return by driving the hips forwards. The spine does not move: the movement comes from the hip.

    3 × 10 · 4 days/week

  4. On your back, hips and knees at 90°. With the transversus activated and the lower back pressed to the floor, slowly lower one heel until it brushes the floor and return. Alternate. If your back lifts off, reduce the range.

    3 × 8 each side · 4 days/week

  5. Sitting on the floor, one leg in front and the other to the side, both knees at 90°. With the trunk upright and hands behind you if needed, rotate the knees from one side to the other without rushing. Look for range, never pain on the side of the hip.

    2 × 8 each side · 4 days/week

  6. One knee on the floor on a cushion and the other foot in front. Tuck the pelvis under, as if bringing the pubic bone towards your navel, and shift your weight forwards until you feel the stretch in the groin of the back leg. Without raising the arm and without arching the lower back.

    3 × 30 s each side · 4 days/week

Phase 3 · Functional strength

Weeks 7 to 12 · 3 days a week · 25 minutes

The goal is for the hip to cope with standing on one leg, slopes and uneven ground. You move on by criterion, not by calendar: two weeks completing Phase 2 without worsening the next day.

  1. Band around the thighs, above the knees. Knees slightly bent and trunk upright. Take short, controlled side steps without the feet coming together or the pelvis tilting.

    3 × 10 steps each way · 3 days/week

  2. With the band on, step diagonally forwards and out with one foot, then the other, and back in the same order, drawing a V. Trunk still.

    3 × 8 · 3 days/week

  3. Facing a low step, 15 to 20 cm. Step up with the whole foot on the step, pushing with the top leg without pushing off with the bottom one. The pelvis stays level and the knee does not cave in. Lower slowly with the same leg.

    3 × 8 each leg · 3 days/week

  4. Standing on one leg, knee slightly bent, next to a support for safety. Keep the pelvis level: the hip of the raised leg must not drop. Progression: arms crossed, eyes closed, soft surface.

    3 × 30 s each leg · 3 days/week

  5. Like the Phase 1 bridge, but with only one foot on the floor and the other leg straight. Pelvis level throughout. If one hip drops, go back to the two-foot bridge for a few more weeks.

    3 × 8 each side · 3 days/week

  6. Knees to chest, 90-90 mobility and psoas stretch. Do not drop them: they are what supports the strength work.

    2 sets of each · every day

What to avoid
  • Crossing your legs when sitting, whether knee over knee or ankle over knee.
  • Sleeping on the more painful hip, or on your side without a pillow between the knees.
  • Standing "hanging" on one hip: spread your weight over both feet.
  • Low chairs and sofas: raise the seat with a cushion.
  • Stretches that cross the leg in front of the body, such as the figure-four piriformis stretch or the iliotibial band stretch: they stretch the tendon right against the bone.
  • In the mountains, walking down long slopes without poles.
Complementary treatment

If pain stops you from progressing, ultrasound in clinic shows the state of the tendons and allows us to consider an ultrasound-guided injection or PRP (platelet-rich plasma). They work best combined with this plan, never instead of it.

When to seek advice before your review

  • Pain running down the leg below the knee, with tingling or numbness.
  • Loss of strength: the leg "gives way" when you stand on it or you drag your foot.
  • Groin pain or a feeling of the hip locking.
  • Night pain that repeatedly wakes you despite sleeping with a pillow between the knees.
  • Pain that increases session after session for more than a week despite reducing the load.

Diffuse discomfort on the side of the hip for 24 hours after a new session, tired glutes and improvement in steps, with plateau weeks, are all normal.
