Exercise plan · Knee

Exercise plan for patellar chondropathy

A guide only. It does not replace a medical assessment or your physiotherapist's judgement. If the pain clearly increases, stop and seek advice.
Why this plan

The kneecap is not a loose bone: it is a pulley. It glides along a groove in the femur every time you bend and straighten the knee, and it is covered by the thickest cartilage in the body. That cartilage does not hurt, because it has no nerves, but when it softens it stops spreading pressure well, and that pressure reaches the bone beneath and the surrounding tissues, which do hurt. That is why it bothers you going down stairs or slopes, getting up from a low chair, squatting or after sitting for a long time with the knee bent.

What an MRI shows does not decide how much it hurts: what decides it is how much load passes through and with what control. Exercise is now the treatment with the strongest evidence for this problem. A strong quadriceps absorbs the load and guides the kneecap down the centre of the groove. And a strong gluteus stops the knee from caving inwards with every step, which is what pulls the kneecap off centre. Programmes that work the hip and knee together give better results than those that work the knee alone.

Cartilage does not improve with rest, and muscle lost in three weeks of protection takes months to recover. Pain is not a sign that you are doing damage, but that this load, today, is too much. The aim is to work below the threshold and gradually raise it.

The golden rule

Load is adjusted according to the pain the following day, not the same day. During exercise, discomfort of up to 4 out of 10 is acceptable. If the next morning the knee is worse than before the session, you overdid it: rest that day and do half at the next session. If you are the same or better, carry on and increase.

Before you start
  • Respect the working ranges. With the foot on the ground (squat, leg press, lunge), between 0 and 45 degrees of bend: beyond that, pressure on the kneecap shoots up. With the leg free (seated extension), the opposite: from 90 to 45 degrees, without straightening fully
  • The load has to go up. From the fourth week, every strength exercise uses a weight with which the last two repetitions are genuinely hard. If you can do 15 without effort, the weight is too light
  • Three sessions a week, not five. That is the frequency used in the studies, and muscle needs the day in between to adapt. More is not better: it is what causes relapse
  • Twelve weeks. Real improvement starts with the strength phase. Giving up in week four because it no longer hurts is the most common reason the problem comes back
  • Cardio from day one. Exercise bike with the saddle high and low resistance, gentle cross-trainer, swimming without breaststroke and walking on the flat. Cartilage is nourished by movement

Phase 1 · Activation and pain control

Weeks 1 to 3 · 5 days a week · 15 minutes

The aim of this phase is not to gain strength but to wake up the quadriceps and bring the pain down enough to be able to train. A knee that has been hurting for a while inhibits the quadriceps reflexively: the muscle is there, but it does not switch on when it should. Everything in this phase is done with hardly any joint movement, which is how the muscle is loaded without compressing the kneecap.

  1. Seated or lying with the leg straight and a rolled towel under the knee. Press the knee into the towel as if trying to flatten it, until you feel the thigh go hard and the kneecap rise slightly. Hold and release slowly. This exercise reactivates the inhibited quadriceps and also eases the pain.

    5 × 45 s (15 s rest) · twice a day, every day

  2. Lying on your back, one knee bent with the foot flat and the other leg straight. First tighten the quadriceps of the straight leg and lift it about 30 cm, without bending the knee at any point. Lower counting 3 seconds. From the second week, with a 1-2 kg ankle weight.

    3 × 10 · tempo 2-1-3 · 5 days/week

  3. Back against the wall, feet 40 cm away from it, slide down until the knees are at 30-45º (not as low as chair height) and hold. The knees do not cave in and the weight is spread across both feet. If it hurts, come up a few centimetres.

    5 × 30 s, progressing to 5 × 45 s · 5 days/week

  4. Lying on your side, hips and knees bent and feet together; from the second week, with a band above the knees. Open the top knee without the pelvis rolling back: if your body moves back, the glute has stopped working. Hold for 2 seconds at the top.

    3 × 15 · 5 days/week

  5. Lying on your side as in the photo, bottom leg bent and top leg straight and slightly back, with the foot facing forwards or slightly down. Lift the leg about 30º and lower slowly, without the pelvis rolling back. With a 1-2 kg ankle weight from the start. It works gluteus medius, which stops the knee caving in when you walk.

    3 × 12 · 5 days/week

  6. On your back, knees bent and feet flat hip-width apart. Lift the pelvis by squeezing the glutes until knee, hip and shoulder are in line; hold for 2 seconds at the top and lower one vertebra at a time. Do not arch the lower back to go higher.

    3 × 12 · 5 days/week

  7. Standing, rest your heel on a step or low bench with the leg straight and the foot relaxed. Lean the trunk forwards from the hip, with a straight back, until you feel tension at the back of the thigh, never pain in the knee. Short hamstrings increase the pressure of the kneecap against the femur with every step.

    3 × 30 s · every day

  8. Standing, cross the affected leg behind the other and shift your hip to that side, leaning the trunk the opposite way. When tight, the iliotibial band pulls the kneecap outwards; it is the structure that pulls it off centre the most.

    3 × 30 s · every day

  9. Standing facing a wall, the leg to stretch behind and the heel on the floor. First with the knee straight (calf), then with the knee slightly bent (soleus). A short calf forces the foot to pronate and the knee ends up caving in.

    3 × 30 s each · every day

Phase 2 · Strength (the core of the programme)

Weeks 4 to 8 · 3 non-consecutive days a week · 30-35 minutes

This is the phase that really changes the knee, and it reproduces the programme with the best results in clinical trials: knee and hip work in the same session, three days a week. The golden rule is tempo and load: 2 seconds up, 1 pause, 3 down, and a weight with which the last two repetitions of each set are hard. Check the weight every week: if 10 repetitions are no longer hard, increase it.

  1. Seated on a machine or with an ankle weight, straighten the knee from 90º and stop at 45º: do not finish the movement by straightening fully, which is where the kneecap takes the most pressure with the leg free. Load: whatever lets you do exactly 10 repetitions, around 70-80 % of your maximum. Lower counting 3 seconds. Note the photo: the knee stays halfway, without straightening fully.

    3 × 10 · tempo 2-1-3 · 3 days/week

  2. On a decline board, hands behind your head and weight spread across the whole foot, go down only to 45º of knee bend and come back up. On a leg press it is the same range, with feet hip-width apart and without the hips lifting off the backrest. The two options are equivalent: the decline board loads the kneecap slightly less. The photo shows the full movement; you stop halfway, at 45º.

    3 × 10 · tempo 2-1-3 · 3 days/week

  3. Standing on the wedge, with a thick band anchored to the wall behind the knees, pulling them backwards. Go down to 45º keeping the trunk upright and come back up. The band offloads the kneecap and focuses the work on the quadriceps: it is the version an irritable knee tolerates best. If you have no band, repeat the decline squat without it.

    3 × 10 · 3 days/week

  4. Standing on the affected leg with the knee slightly bent, tilt the trunk forwards while the other leg rises behind in the same line, with a straight back. Lower counting 4 seconds and return. It works the hamstring and hip control at the same time: the hamstrings balance the quadriceps' pull on the knee.

    3 × 8 each leg · 4 s lowering · 2 days/week

  5. Progression of the Phase 1 abduction. Resting on your forearm and knee or foot, lift the hips until ankle, hip and shoulder are in line and raise the top leg about 30º. The hip is half the treatment: programmes that work only the knee lose their effect at one year. If you cannot yet control it, keep doing the side-lying abduction with a 3-4 kg ankle weight.

    3 × 10 each side · 3 days/week

  6. Band above the knees or at the ankles, half squat, and move sideways without bringing the feet together and without the knees caving in. The trunk does not lean.

    3 × 12 steps each way · 3 days/week

  7. Standing and holding a support, take the straight leg backwards by squeezing the glute, without arching your back to gain range. Return slowly. When the movement is easy, add an elastic band at the ankle.

    3 × 10 · 3 days/week

  8. Glute bridge with only one foot on the floor and the other leg straight in the air. The pelvis must not drop towards the free side. It is the intermediate step before the single-leg work of Phase 3.

    3 × 10 each side · 3 days/week

  9. Hamstrings, calf and soleus, and tensor fasciae latae. Done at the end of the session, never before the strength part.

    3 × 30 s each · every day

Phase 3 · Advanced strength and movement control

Weeks 9 to 12 · 3 days a week · 35-40 minutes

The aim now is for the knee to cope with what real life asks of it: stairs, slopes, getting up carrying something and, if relevant, running again. Load goes up, single-leg work begins and the range that had been limited is gradually recovered. You do not move into this phase by calendar but by criterion: two weeks completing Phase 2 without the knee worsening the next day.

  1. The same range from 90º to 45º, now with a weight that only allows 8 clean repetitions, around 80 % of your maximum. It is the exercise that builds the most strength and the one worth keeping when the programme ends.

    4 × 8 · tempo 2-1-3 · 3 days/week

  2. The same decline squat, now with dumbbells and extending the range to 60º as long as it is pain-free. If pain appears at 60º, go back to 45º for another week and try again.

    4 × 8 · 3 days/week

  3. Step forwards lowering the back knee, without the front knee bending beyond 60º or caving in. Start on firm ground; the unstable surface in the photo is the progression, only when the movement is perfect.

    3 × 8 each leg · 3 days/week

  4. In a half squat, diagonal steps forwards and back drawing a V, keeping the knee aligned over the foot with every step and without the trunk rising. When you control it, add a band above the knees.

    3 × 10 each side · 3 days/week

  5. Now with the foot on a raised surface or with weight on the pelvis. Hold for 2 seconds at the top.

    3 × 10 each side · 3 days/week

  6. It continues, now with a dumbbell in the opposite hand and a slower lowering.

    3 × 8 each leg · 2 days/week

Return to running
  • Return to running. From week 9, if you can go down stairs without pain and cope with Phase 3 without worsening. Alternate 1 minute running and 2 walking up to 20 minutes, on the flat and on alternate days, and increase by 10 % a week
  • Cadence. Shorter, more frequent steps, between 5 and 10 % more, immediately reduce the load on the kneecap
  • Two details. If your foot pronates a lot, an off-the-shelf insole for the first weeks helps while you build strength. And every kilo lost is four kilos less pressure on the kneecap when climbing stairs
What to avoid
  • In the first weeks: going down stairs and slopes, deep squats, sitting for long periods with the knee bent and running. They come back when strength can support them.
  • Working outside the ranges of each exercise.
  • Strength training on consecutive days.
  • Staying on the same exercise for months without increasing the weight.
Complementary treatment

If pain is still limiting after completing the strength phases, examination and ultrasound in clinic help decide whether an ultrasound-guided injection or PRP (platelet-rich plasma) is advisable. They never replace strength work.

When to seek advice before your review

  • Knee locking: it gets stuck and you cannot straighten it fully.
  • Swelling that appears after sessions and does not go down within 24 hours.
  • A feeling that the knee "gives way", especially going down stairs.
  • Pain that repeatedly wakes you at night.
  • Pain that increases session after session for more than a week despite reducing the load.

Diffuse discomfort for 24-48 hours after a new session or a weight increase, muscle soreness in the quadriceps and painless crunching when bending the knee are all normal: noise on its own does not indicate damage. Improvement comes in steps, with plateaus of one or two weeks.
