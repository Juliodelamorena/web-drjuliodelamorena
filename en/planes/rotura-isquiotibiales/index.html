Exercise plan · Thigh

Rehabilitation plan for a hamstring tear

A guide only. It does not replace a medical assessment or your physiotherapist's judgement. If the pain clearly increases, stop and seek advice.
Why this plan

A healing muscle needs progressive load so that the scar lines up properly and stays elastic. Too little load leaves a weak scar, and too much too soon tears it open again. That is why it goes in phases: first strength without movement, then with movement and finally braking the weight.

You start when ultrasound confirms that the edges of the tear have joined. Until then, compression, ice several times a day and no hamstring work. Each phase has a criterion for moving on to the next: you progress by criterion, not by calendar.

The golden rule

Discomfort of up to 3 out of 10 during exercise is acceptable if the next day you are the same or better. A sharp twinge, pain above 4 or new swelling: stop, go back to the previous level and get it checked.

Before you start
  • When to start. Only when ultrasound confirms the tear is closing. Before that, a compression thigh sleeve, ice and no hamstring work
  • Exercise bike. 15-20 minutes without resistance from the first phase
  • Moving between phases. Phase 1 → 2: isometrics at maximum effort without pain. Phase 2 → 3: Swiss-ball curl and deadlift without pain, and a stretch with the same range as the healthy leg
  • Warm up before running. At least 15 minutes of progressive running and accelerations before any sprint

Phase 1 · Isometric

Week 1 after the ultrasound · every day

Contraction without moving the leg, plus an exercise bike without resistance for 15-20 minutes.

  1. Seated or lying, with the knee slightly bent. Dig your heel into the floor or couch without moving the leg. Start gently.

    5 repetitions · 30-45 seconds · 50-70 % intensity

  2. The same exercise, changing the knee bend. Increase the intensity only if it does not hurt.

    3 repetitions of 30 seconds at 30, 60 and 90 degrees of knee bend

  3. Lying down, knees bent. Lift the pelvis by squeezing the glutes, without arching your back.

    3 × 10 · hold 5 seconds at the top

Phase 2 · Concentric

Week 2 · alternate days

Controlled movement, without bouncing. Gentle stretch at the end.

  1. Heels on the ball, lift the pelvis and hold for 3 seconds.

    3 × 10

  2. With the pelvis raised, bring the ball towards you by bending the knees and return slowly.

    3 × 8-10

  3. Knees slightly bent and back straight. Push the hips back and lower the dumbbells close to your legs until you feel tension, no further.

    3 × 10 · light weight

  4. Lying down, towel around the sole of the foot. Raise the straight leg until you feel gentle tension.

    3 × 30 seconds · pain-free

Phase 3 · Eccentric and running

Weeks 3 and 4 · alternate days for strength

On the days in between, progressive running.

  1. Lying face down with an ankle weight or band: lift with both legs and lower with the injured leg alone over 4 seconds.

    3 × 8

  2. Kneeling with the ankles held, let yourself fall forwards as slowly as possible. Brake with the hamstrings.

    3 × 5 · with help from a band or partner

  3. Standing on the injured leg, tilt the trunk forwards with the other leg behind and the back straight.

    3 × 8 each leg

  4. Lift in 1 second and lower in 3. Increase the weight when the last set feels easy.

    3 × 10

Return to sport
  • Progressive running. On the days in between during Phase 3: gentle jogging for 10-15 minutes and, if pain-free, straight runs at 60, 70 and 80 % of speed. No sprinting or kicking yet
  • Return to sport. From week 5 and after review: progressive sprinting two days a week, changes of direction and kicking built up gradually, and training with the team, first without contact
  • Before the first match. At least two full training sessions without discomfort
  • Maintenance. The Nordic curl and single-leg deadlift twice a week throughout the season: the best protection against another tear
What to avoid
  • Stretching hard or doing hamstring work before the tear has closed.
  • Sprints and kicking before you can do straight runs at 80 % without pain.
  • Skipping phases because you feel good.
  • Dropping the Nordic curl once you are back playing.
Complementary treatment

Ultrasound in clinic shows how the tear is closing and helps decide the timing of each phase. In some muscle injuries, ultrasound-guided PRP (platelet-rich plasma) is considered.

When to seek advice before your review

  • You feel a sharp twinge during an exercise or a run.
  • A new bruise appears or the area swells.
  • The pain does not ease within 48 hours.

It is normal for the muscle to feel tired or tight after new sessions and to have some soreness when you start the eccentrics.
