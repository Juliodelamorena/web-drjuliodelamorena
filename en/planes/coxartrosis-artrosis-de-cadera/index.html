Exercise plan · Hip

Exercise plan for hip osteoarthritis

A guide only. It does not replace a medical assessment or your physiotherapist's judgement. If the pain clearly increases, stop and seek advice.
Why this plan

In hip osteoarthritis, the pain is often greater than the imaging explains. That gap is good news: much of the pain does not come from the bone but from the muscles around the hip, which have spent months working badly to protect it. The gluteus medius weakens, the psoas and groin muscles tighten to "hold" the joint, and that sustained tension is what wakes you at night and makes you limp.

Any treatment that eases the pain, such as an injection, opens a window of weeks with less pain. That window is the opportunity: if you use it to strengthen the glutes and adductors, the hip is better supported and the relief lasts much longer. If you do not, the pain returns when the effect wears off. That is why this plan is not an extra: it is half of the treatment.

The golden rule

Load is adjusted according to the pain the following day, not the same day. If the next morning the hip hurts more than before the session, you overdid it: rest that day and do half at the next session. If you are the same or better, carry on and increase. Discomfort of up to 3 out of 10 during exercise is acceptable if it goes away when you finish.

Before you start
  • Warm up on an exercise bike. 8-10 minutes at low resistance with the saddle high, so that the knee is almost straight at the bottom: a low saddle closes the hip. The circular movement without load lubricates the joint
  • The phases overlap. When you start a new phase, the exercises from the previous one become your warm-up. You move on by criterion, not by calendar: one or two weeks without worsening the next day
  • Walk every day. On flat ground and without seeking fatigue. On days without strength work, 30-40 minutes of cycling or swimming without breaststroke
  • Raise your seat. If your knee is higher than your hip when you sit, the joint closes and gets pinched. Use a cushion on low chairs and sofas

Phase 0 · Settle and activate

Week 0 · every day · 10-15 minutes

If you have had a procedure on the hip, for the next 3-4 days just normal life and short walks. Then start here: sustained contractions without moving the joint. They have an immediate pain-relieving effect and wake up muscles that have been switched off for a while.

  1. Lying on your back, knees bent. Breathe in, sending the air to your abdomen and sides; as you breathe out, gently draw your navel towards your spine. It helps release tension in the groin.

    5 minutes · every day

  2. On your back, knees bent and together. Let them drop slowly to one side and then the other, without lifting your shoulders. Short range: if you feel pinching in the groin, reduce it.

    2 × 10 each side · every day

  3. On your back, knees bent, elastic band just above the knees. Push the knees outwards against the band without actually moving them, and hold. Moderate intensity, without shaking.

    5 × 30-45 s · 1 min rest · every day

  4. Same position, with a ball or cushion between the knees. Squeeze it steadily at 50 % of your strength. If the groin complains, ease the pressure.

    5 × 20-30 s · every day

Phase 1 · Unloaded strength

Weeks 1 and 2 · 5 days a week · 20 minutes

Now the joint moves, lying down and without weight. Slow movements: 2 seconds up, 1 pause, 3 down. The Phase 0 isometrics become your warm-up. Criterion to start: one week of Phase 0 without worsening the next day.

  1. On your back, knees bent, band above the knees. Push the knees against the band and lift the pelvis until knees, hips and shoulders are in line. Pause at the top and lower slowly, without arching your back.

    3 × 10 · 3 s pause at the top

  2. Lying on your side on the hip that does not hurt, pillow between the knees, hips and knees bent to about 45°. With heels together, open the top knee without letting the pelvis roll back. Add a band when 15 repetitions are easy.

    3 × 12-15 each side

  3. Lying face down with a pillow under the abdomen. With the knee bent to 90°, lift the thigh a few centimetres off the floor by squeezing the glute, without arching the lower back.

    3 × 10 each side

  4. Lying on your side on the hip you are working, with the top leg bent and resting in front. Lift the straight bottom leg a few centimetres and lower slowly.

    3 × 10-12 each side

Phase 2 · Standing load

Weeks 3 to 6 · 4 days a week · 25 minutes

Now the hip has to support you standing. This is the phase that changes your walking and limp the most. Criterion to start: two weeks of Phase 1 without worsening the next day.

  1. Band above the knees, knees slightly bent and trunk upright. Short, slow side steps, without bringing the feet together or tilting the pelvis.

    3 × 10 steps each way

  2. Standing facing a support, knees slightly bent. Push the hips back, tilting the trunk with a straight back, and return by driving the hips forwards. Only go as far as you feel no pinching in the groin.

    3 × 10

  3. Back against the wall, feet hip-width apart and a step forward. Slide down without going beyond 60° of knee bend and come back up. Never deeper.

    3 × 10

  4. Standing, holding a support. Take the straight leg backwards by squeezing the glute, without arching your back or rotating the pelvis. With a band on the ankle when it becomes easy.

    3 × 12 each side

  5. Next to a support for safety, on the leg you are working with the knee slightly bent. The pelvis must stay level: the hip of the raised leg does not drop.

    3 × 30 s each leg

Phase 3 · Functional strength

Weeks 7 to 12 · 3 days a week · 30 minutes

The goal is for the hip to cope with daily life without paying for it the next day: stairs, slopes and long periods on your feet. Criterion to start: two weeks of Phase 2 without worsening.

  1. Low step, 15 to 20 cm. Step up with the whole foot on the step, pushing with the top leg without pushing off with the bottom one. Pelvis level and knee in line with the foot. Lower slowly.

    3 × 8 each leg

  2. Like the Phase 1 bridge, with only one foot on the floor and the other leg straight. The pelvis must not drop towards the raised leg; if it does, go back to the two-foot bridge for a few weeks.

    3 × 8 each side

  3. With the band on, step diagonally forwards and out with one foot, then the other, and back in the same order. Trunk still.

    3 × 8

  4. On your side, resting on your forearm with the knee of the top leg on a low bench. Lift the hips and hold. Only if the groin does not complain; if it does, go back to side-lying adduction.

    3 × 15-20 s each side

Stretches · Every day

At the end of every session. No bouncing and never into pain.

  1. Back knee resting on a cushion. Tuck the pelvis under, as if bringing the pubic bone towards your navel, and shift your weight forwards until you feel the stretch in the groin. Do not arch your back; the arm can stay down.

    3 × 30 s each side

  2. Seated, with one leg straight out to the side and the other bent. Lean the trunk forwards with a straight back until you feel tension on the inner thigh.

    3 × 30 s each side

What to avoid
  • Crossing your legs and sitting on low chairs or sofas.
  • Deep squats, crouching or sitting back on your heels.
  • Pivoting on the standing leg with the trunk twisted: when you turn, move your feet.
  • For now, straight-leg raises lying on your back or bringing the knee to the chest against resistance: they load the psoas directly.
  • Running, jumping, sports with changes of direction and breaststroke.
  • On stairs, go up with the good leg and down with the bad one, holding the handrail. When sleeping, lie on your back or on your side with a thick pillow between the knees.
Complementary treatment

When pain prevents you from doing the plan, an ultrasound-guided injection can open that window of weeks with less pain; in some cases PRP (platelet-rich plasma) is considered. This is decided in clinic based on examination and imaging. None of it replaces strength work.

When to seek advice before your review

  • The hip locks, or you feel a snap with severe pain and the leg "catches".
  • Night pain starts waking you again several nights in a row.
  • You cannot put weight on the leg, or the limp clearly worsens.
  • Pain increases session after session for more than a week despite reducing the load.

It is normal for the glutes to feel tired, to have diffuse discomfort for 24 hours after a new session and for improvement to come in steps, with plateau weeks.
