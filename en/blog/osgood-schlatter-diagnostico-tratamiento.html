Osgood-Schlatter: diagnosis and treatment

Osgood-Schlatter disease is a traction apophysitis of the tibial tuberosity and the most common cause of knee pain in growing young athletes. It is diagnosed with an examination and an ultrasound, without radiation, and conservative treatment resolves more than nine out of ten cases. Dr Julio De la Morena assesses it at Hospital Memorial Publio Cordón (Pozuelo de Alarcón, Madrid).

The video

What it is

It is a traction apophysitis of the tibial tuberosity: the patellar tendon pulls on an area of bone that is still growing. It mainly affects teenagers during periods of rapid growth and is the most common cause of knee pain in skeletally immature athletes (Musculoskeletal Surgery, 2026).

What the ultrasound shows

The patellar tendon of the pain-free side is compared with the affected side. On the affected side the tibial tuberosity appears fragmented and Doppler shows more vascular signal, which corresponds to inflammation. Ultrasound uses no radiation. Reviews recommend using imaging judiciously: to confirm the diagnosis or rule out other causes (Southern Medical Journal, 2026).

Conservative treatment

It consists of adjusting activity, progressive rehabilitation and control of training load. With this approach, outcomes are good in more than nine out of ten paediatric cases (Musculoskeletal Surgery, 2026).

How long it can last

It resolves with growth, but symptoms can last up to 24 months, and around 10% of those affected keep some discomfort as adults, especially when kneeling. That is why good load control during growth matters.

When to seek an assessment

For pain below the kneecap that appears with sport, a painful bump under the kneecap, or pain that does not improve when activity is adjusted. An examination and an ultrasound can confirm the diagnosis and rule out other causes.

Frequently asked questions

What is Osgood-Schlatter disease?

An inflammation of the area of the tibia where the patellar tendon attaches, common in teenagers who play sport while growing.

Is an X-ray needed?

Not always. Examination and ultrasound, which uses no radiation, are usually enough; MRI or X-ray are kept for doubtful cases.

Does it go away?

Conservative treatment gives good results in more than nine out of ten cases. Symptoms can last up to 24 months and settle when growth ends.

Do you recognise yourself in what you have just read?

A full assessment and a clear plan at your first visit; ultrasound and treatment the same day when indicated. The first consultation does not commit you to treatment: if no treatment is indicated, we will tell you.
