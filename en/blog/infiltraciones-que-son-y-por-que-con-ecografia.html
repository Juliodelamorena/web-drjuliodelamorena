Injections: what they are and why they should be ultrasound-guided

An injection means delivering a treatment directly into the area that hurts. When it is guided by ultrasound, the needle is seen in real time and the treatment reaches the exact spot. This is what the studies say, with their links and also their limits.

The video

What an injection is

An injection means delivering a treatment directly into the area that hurts: a joint, a tendon, a ligament or a muscle.

What can be injected

The most common are PRP (platelet-rich plasma), hyaluronic acid, corticosteroids and local anaesthetic. Less often, bone marrow concentrate, prolotherapy or botulinum toxin are used. The choice depends on the diagnosis and on what is indicated in each case.

Does it hurt?

The discomfort is mild and similar to that of a blood test. Afterwards, the patient walks home.

Why it should be ultrasound-guided

With the ultrasound machine, the needle and the structure being treated are seen in real time, so the treatment reaches exactly where it needs to go and blood vessels and nerves are avoided.

A review of 12 randomised trials concluded that ultrasound-guided intra-articular and periarticular injections are more accurate than those guided by anatomical landmarks (odds ratio 0.36) and reduce pain at 2 and 6 weeks; at 12 weeks the difference was no longer significant (American Journal of Physical Medicine & Rehabilitation, 2015). Another review of four trials in knee osteoarthritis, with 338 patients, found better pain and better function with ultrasound guidance (Australasian Journal of Ultrasound in Medicine, 2024).

What these studies do not say

In the shoulder, a Cochrane review of five trials with corticosteroids found no clinical advantage of ultrasound over landmark-guided injection (2012). The greater accuracy is consistent; whether it translates into a greater and lasting clinical benefit depends on the area and the treatment, and is still being studied.
